# Optics, not shaders, hide the grid

The renderer is already at or above what Virtual Desktop-class apps do for shrinking a picture, so the largest remaining gains sit outside the downscale filter: in the optics, in a few renderer details nobody else on Cardboard does (linear-light filtering, per-channel chromatic-aberration correction), and in spending the encoder's bits on pixels the headset can actually show. An S22 Ultra behind Cardboard lenses delivers about **13 pixels per degree** (970 px across 75°), the same class as Gear VR and the first Rift, roughly two-thirds of a Quest 2; at that density the pixel grid is four times coarser than the eye resolves and **no software change can remove it**. Every manufacturer that reduced screen-door effect did it with an optical low-pass at the panel and accepted a sharpness cost, which is the same trade the user already chose in software (softness 80%, no sharpening). The stream settings in use (HEVC P4, spatial AQ, VBV 100, 150 Mbps) are already beyond the documented defaults and beyond what creators and issue reporters run; the remaining encoder levers are 10-bit, two-pass and GPU headroom, not bitrate. The one open question worth a careful test is stream resolution: the project's own screenshots show the top 4K mip level contributes nothing at the chosen softness, which is exactly what the outside evidence predicts, but it is presented here as an A/B test, not a recommendation. The report closes with a ranked list of fifteen experiments wired to the `debug.vrdesktop.*` harness.

**Evidence quality, stated plainly.** No researcher could read Reddit: direct fetches were refused, mirrors returned HTTP 403 or reset the connection, and the in-app browser blocks the site. Every Reddit-derived point below is a **search-result summary only** and is marked "(Reddit, summary only)". YouTube caption downloads were rate-limited (HTTP 429) part-way through; 21 videos were read, and the only two that appeared to contain measured Moonlight image-quality comparisons were not among them. Creator claims are almost all asserted, not measured. GitHub issues, official docs, Meta developer pages and open-source shader code were read directly and carry the most weight. No source tested a diffuser, lens swap or PenTile-aware rendering on a phone in a Cardboard viewer; that part rests on vendor practice, one 2014-era forum thread and geometry. Statements marked "inference" are reasoning from these sources, not findings.

## Thirteen pixels per degree sets a ceiling software cannot lift

Screen-door visibility is fixed by angular pixel pitch, the unlit gap between emitters, and subpixel layout ([LaValle, Virtual Reality §5.4](https://lavalle.pl/vr/node149.html)). The project's own measurement, 970 px across a 75° screen, gives 12.9 pixels per degree. Quest 2 is **20 PPD**, Quest 3 **25 PPD**, and retinal resolution is put at about 60 ([Meta, "Stacking the Optical Deck"](https://www.meta.com/blog/vr-display-optics-pancake-lenses-ppd/)); the Vive, Rift CV1 and PSVR generation sat at 10 to 15 ([xVR wiki](https://xvrwiki.org/wiki/Pixel_density)). The phone is therefore a first-generation headset in angular terms, and each pixel subtends over four arcminutes against the eye's one.

Diamond PenTile makes this worse for some colours. Each pixel has a green emitter but only half a red and half a blue ([VR/AR wiki, PenTile](https://vrarwiki.com/wiki/PenTile)), and PSVR2 reviewers report a blurrier image "especially in the red and blue areas" ([MIXED](https://mixed-news.com/en/psvr-2-display-has-fewer-subpixels-per-pixel-than-the-psvr/)). By geometry (inference) the red and blue lattices are about 1.4 times coarser than green, so saturated red or blue regions show a coarser dot pattern than white or green ones. That matters here because **Color is set to 120%**: pushing saturation drives exactly the sparse emitters harder and lets the dense green lattice go dark in red and blue areas. No source measured this on a phone, so it is a cheap in-headset test, not a conclusion.

What every shipped fix has in common is that it is optical. Samsung's Odyssey+ used a diffusing grid over the panel ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/samsungs-odyssey-vr-headset-claims-1233-ppi-user-perceived-resolution/)); the Valve Index has a diffuser, and Road to VR notes "you of course sacrifice sharpness when using a diffuser" ([Road to VR](https://roadtovr.com/valve-index-teardown-disassembly-lenses-optics-diffuser-ipd-mechansim/)); PSVR2 has a diffusion coating "at a small cost in sharpness" ([Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/?p=3296455)). The DIY record is one Rift DK2 thread: laminating pouches removed the grid but were "very blurry" or sparkly, while a single layer of **3M Vikuiti film** softened the grid with clarity kept and needed a gamma or contrast lift ([Oculus developer forum](https://communityforums.atmeta.com/discussions/dev-pcvr/screen-door-effect-mod/137595)). On a phone the film sits on the cover glass, much further from the emitters than on a bare DK2 panel, so it will blur more for the same film (inference); strength must be found by trial, and coarse matte protectors are a risk because their sparkle is itself a fixed pattern once magnified. Slight defocus is the zero-cost optical equivalent. Longer focal-length lenses (45 mm against 40 mm) raise pixels per degree in proportion, about 12%, at the cost of field of view; they are the only lever that raises true angular density. A single anecdote says a Note 5 looked "a lot crisper with barely any screen door effect" in a cheap Cardboard than in Gear VR, plausibly because of lower magnification (Reddit, summary only: [r/GearVR listing](https://pol1.lr.ggtyler.dev/r/GearVR/comments/3x5k29/note_5_with_google_cardboard_is_significantly)).

Software's role is narrower: avoid making the grid more conspicuous and avoid adding shimmer of its own. Sources call higher render resolution only "marginally" helpful ([VR Vision Group](https://www.vrvisiongroup.com/?p=3374)). PenTile "checkerboarding" shows in high-contrast areas ([Oculus developer forum, PenTile vs RGB](https://communityforums.atmeta.com/discussions/dev-pcvr/screendoor-effect-pentile-vs-rgb/107684)), which is consistent with the user's rejection of sharpening. The lab technique that does beat the grid, physically shifting the display 10 µm at 120 Hz, moves the emitters themselves ([Road to VR](https://roadtovr.com/?p=170180)); shader jitter moves only the image, so it cannot substitute. Gear VR's low-persistence mode is gone: Samsung removed Gear VR support with Android 12, which the S22 launched on ([XDA](https://www.xda-developers.com/samsung-gear-vr-stop-working-android-12-update/)), and low persistence addresses motion smear rather than the grid in any case. One user in the PenTile thread offers the counterweight to all of this: "Screendoor is less distracting than the low detail of low resolution." Preferences differ, which is why the split view exists.

## The renderer already out-filters Quest apps, so the gaps are elsewhere

On Quest the standard answer to a 4K picture on a smaller virtual screen is not a clever kernel. Apps hand the frame to the system compositor and ask for "supersampling": a two-tap filter that alternates its pattern every other frame, or a static four-tap filter ([Meta, Composition Layer Filtering](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/native/android/mobile-openxr-composition-layer-filtering)). Moonlight XR, the Quest fork of Moonlight, does exactly this and samples the video itself with plain `GL_LINEAR` and no mipmaps ([xr_layers.c](https://github.com/Gilleece/moonlight-android-xr/blob/HEAD/app/src/main/jni/xr-renderer/xr_layers.c), [xr_gl.c](https://github.com/Gilleece/moonlight-android-xr/blob/HEAD/app/src/main/jni/xr-renderer/xr_gl.c)). ALVR's stream sampler is linear with no mip filter or anisotropy ([stream.rs](https://github.com/alvr-org/ALVR/blob/master/alvr/graphics/src/stream.rs)). This project's mipmaps, trilinear filtering, anisotropy and four-tap rotated-grid supersample are the same class as Meta's quality mode with a proper prefilter underneath. Virtual Desktop, Bigscreen, Skybox and Steam Link are closed source, and no primary statement of their sampling path was found; nothing in the evidence suggests a bicubic or Lanczos minifier would be visible. The user's soft preference matches the desktop-video school, where mpv's quality profile downscales with Mitchell and Hermite is praised for artifact-free softness ([guide.encode.moe](https://guide.encode.moe/encoding/resampling.html)).

The sharpeners these apps advertise are the wrong tool here. Snapdragon Game Super Resolution is "a 12-tap Lanczos-like scaling filter and adaptive sharpening filter" for upscaling ([SGSR v1 README](https://github.com/SnapdragonStudios/snapdragon-gsr/blob/main/sgsr/v1/README.md)); Meta warns its own SGSR-based sharpener adds flicker on content near display resolution ([Meta blog, MQSR](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/vr-image-quality-meta-quest-super-resolution)). Creators independently arrive where the user did: Virtual Desktop's sharpening runs after decoding and at 100% "brings out the compression" ([Virtually Real, "my quick overview of Virtual Desktops new Snapdragon game super resolution"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sZWx3iJ0Ets&t=30s)), and a 2025 guide caps it at 75% ([F4CEpa1m, "Best Virtual Desktop Setup + Settings"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=0MXLMEUHRc0&t=1140s)). ALVR sharpens on the server for the same reason ([ALVR settings tutorial](https://github.com/alvr-org/ALVR/wiki/Settings-tutorial)). These filters become relevant only if the stream ever drops to or below the on-screen pixel count.

Three techniques are adoptable and untried. First, **linear-light filtering**. Carmack's guidance for VR video is blunt: "Use an sRGB format swapchain and framebuffer to get gamma-correct filtering" ([Meta blog, Techniques for Improved VR Video](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/techniques-for-improved-vr-video-w-john-carmack/)), and mpv's quality profile enables linear downscaling ([mpv builtin.conf](https://git.tjdev.de/mirror/mpv/src/branch/rc/etc/builtin.conf)). Averaging gamma-encoded values dims fine high-contrast detail ([guide.encode.moe](https://guide.encode.moe/encoding/resampling.html)), and the effect grows with the downscale ratio, which here is three to four. Neither ALVR nor Moonlight XR does it ([stream.wgsl](https://github.com/alvr-org/ALVR/blob/master/alvr/graphics/resources/stream.wgsl), [xr_gl.c](https://github.com/Gilleece/moonlight-android-xr/blob/HEAD/app/src/main/jni/xr-renderer/xr_gl.c)), so the effect is evidently not glaring on game footage; thin text, pitch lines and HUD numerals are where it should show. On the SDR path an `GL_SRGB8_ALPHA8` intermediate texture makes mip generation, trilinear, anisotropy and the supersample average all run in linear light at no shader cost (inference from GLES behaviour); the caveat that linear light suits soft kernels ([Plex forum](https://forums.plex.tv/t/video-playback-quality-options/836791)) favours the current settings.

Second, **per-channel chromatic-aberration correction**. Google's Cardboard distortion is a 40×40 mesh sampled with one `texture2D` tap in mediump with a single set of coefficients for all colours; the word "chromatic" does not appear in its sources ([distortion_mesh.cc](https://github.com/googlevr/cardboard/blob/master/sdk/distortion_mesh.cc), [opengl_es2_distortion_renderer.cc](https://github.com/googlevr/cardboard/blob/master/sdk/rendering/opengl_es2_distortion_renderer.cc)). The standard correction resamples red, green and blue at slightly different radial scales ([Meta forum, Gear VR distortion](https://communityforums.atmeta.com/discussions/dev-general/how-does-the-gear-vr-runtime-handle-lens-distortion--chromatic-aberration/613517)). Single-element plastic lenses fringe towards the screen edges, viewer profiles carry no per-channel data, so the red and blue scale factors need hand calibration against a white grid. No source quantifies the fringe for Cardboard lenses; this is the highest-uncertainty, potentially high-reward renderer item.

Third, **frame-alternating taps**. Meta's two-tap alternating filter is "indistinguishable from the four-tap filter" for most uses ([Meta, Composition Layer Filtering](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/native/android/mobile-openxr-composition-layer-filtering)). On a 120 Hz panel the same trick buys either half the texture fetches or eight effective taps for the cost of four. It adds no information the mip chain lacks, so treat it as a performance or smoothness experiment rather than a clarity one.

Screen size and shape are the remaining client levers. A Quest 3 text-clarity test found that enlarging and curving the virtual screen "helped more than anything else", while maxing bitrate made no significant difference ([SpookyFairy VR, "Using Quest 3 as a Monitor"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zgckHCs9nfg&t=90s)). A larger screen puts more display pixels under the same 4K frame; the cost on Cardboard optics is that more of the picture lands in the lens's weaker outer zone.

## At 150 Mbps the encoder is not the bottleneck; headroom and bit depth are

Sunshine's documented defaults are tuned for latency: preset P1, quarter-resolution two-pass, spatial AQ off, VBV increase 0 ([Sunshine configuration docs](https://docs.lizardbyte.dev/projects/sunshine/latest/md_docs_2configuration.html)). The setup here already runs P4 with spatial AQ and VBV 100 at 150 Mbps, the same figure a sim-racing user settled on for 4K60 HEVC ([Sunshine #1998](https://github.com/SunshineStream/Sunshine/issues/1998)). Creators' bitrate advice ranges from "never above 40" to 300 Mbps with no measurements ([Retro Game Corps](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RiggvcusEuA&t=960s), [Joey's Retro Handhelds](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H0jmqVIhwIA&t=750s)); the one creator who measured found that beyond roughly 100 to 120 Mbps more bits bought latency and instability, not visible quality, on a Quest 3 with AV1 ([Mac In VR, "PERFECT Virtual Desktop Settings"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zUaxhvonsAg&t=240s)). Raising bitrate further is not on the list.

The codec is settled by hardware. Turing NVENC cannot encode AV1 ([NVIDIA support matrix](https://developer.nvidia.com/video-encode-decode-support-matrix)), the Snapdragon 8 Gen 1 has no AV1 hardware decoder ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2022/02/18/qualcomm-snapdragon-av1/)), and recombined 4:4:4 decoding "won't be coming to moonlight-android any time soon" ([LizardByte discussion #220](https://github.com/orgs/LizardByte/discussions/220)). HEVC 4:2:0 it stays. Reading Sunshine's source shows lookahead, temporal AQ, AQ strength and reference count are fixed in code, and no fork exposes them ([Sunshine repository](https://github.com/LizardByte/Sunshine)); a custom encoder would add nothing over patching Sunshine, which already drives the NVENC SDK directly.

Two documented levers remain. The current **single-pass** setting is the one choice below Sunshine's default: the docs say disabling two-pass "can lead to occasional bitrate overshoot and subsequent packet loss", and quarter-resolution two-pass improves motion detection and bit distribution ([Sunshine configuration docs](https://docs.lizardbyte.dev/projects/sunshine/latest/md_docs_2configuration.html)). And **10-bit**: Sunshine sends an SDR desktop as 8-bit Rec. 709 unless 10-bit is negotiated ([Sunshine PR #5106](https://github.com/SunshineStream/Sunshine/pull/5106)), while Virtual Desktop users treat 10-bit HEVC or AV1 as the picture-quality choice and report visible banding with 8-bit H.264 ([Mac In VR](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=zUaxhvonsAg&t=510s)). The phone then applies gamma 2.4 and 120% colour on top of the 8-bit signal, which stretches dark steps further apart (inference), so banding is the artefact most likely to respond. Full HDR is a different matter: Apollo's maintainer writes that "Enabling HDR is generally not recommended with ANY streaming solutions at this moment" and that most Android devices make precise calibration impossible ([Apollo README](https://github.com/ClassicOldSong/Apollo#about-hdr), [Artemis #372](https://github.com/ClassicOldSong/moonlight-android/issues/372)).

Encoder time matters more than encoder settings. NVIDIA's throughput figures for a Turing engine imply P1 at 4K should take a few milliseconds ([NVENC application note](https://docs.nvidia.com/video-technologies/video-codec-sdk/13.0/nvenc-application-note/index.html)), and P5 to P7 at 4K are marginal for 60 fps (scaled estimate). When the game saturates the GPU, encode time balloons: an Apollo user saw host processing exceed 160 ms at 99% GPU load, fixed only by a frame limit ([Apollo #942](https://github.com/ClassicOldSong/Apollo/issues/942)). The 120 Hz desktop with a 60 fps stream is the exact 2:1 arrangement Apollo creates on purpose with its "double refresh rate" option, and it works when the game is capped at exactly the stream rate; the maintainer's wiki says a frame limiter "is always better than VSync" ([Apollo Stuttering Clinic](https://github.com/ClassicOldSong/Apollo/wiki/Stuttering-Clinic)), though Sunshine #1998 users found V-sync smoother, so this too is an A/B. The same wiki explains repeated capture restarts, which this project has seen in FC 27: displays, cables or switches that re-probe the connection make the GPU reinitialise and the stream with it. Nothing was found tying EA anti-cheat to capture restarts, and nothing streaming-specific exists for FC or Forza. Finally, shimmer that originates in the game (no temporal anti-aliasing, in-game or driver sharpening) survives any encoder and any downscale; the earlier encoder research ranked in-game anti-aliasing and removing every sharpener as the largest host-side wins, and nothing in the new notes contradicts that.

## Stream resolution is the one open question the evidence cannot settle

The outside evidence leans against 4K for a 970-pixel screen but does not prove it. Carmack's rule for VR video is not to exceed what the display resolves and to spend "the bitrate on better pixels" ([Meta blog](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/blog/techniques-for-improved-vr-video-w-john-carmack/)). Against strict matching, text legibility on Quest 3 reportedly kept improving as the virtual monitor rose from 1080p to 1440p to 1728p, well above the pixels the screen covers (Reddit, summary only: [r/virtualreality thread](https://red.bismuth.it/r/virtualreality/comments/1857125/how_much_resolution_can_quest_3_actually_display)). Together these support moderate oversampling, roughly 1.3 to 2 times the on-screen width, and no controlled comparison of "4K downscaled" against "matched resolution at the same bitrate" exists.

For this headset the arithmetic is as follows (bits per pixel at 150 Mbps and 60 fps; oversampling measured against 970 px):

| Stream | Bits per pixel | Luma oversampling | Chroma width (4:2:0) | Encode and decode load |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3840×2160 | 0.30 | 4.0× | 1920 = 2.0× | 100% |
| 2560×1440 | 0.68 | 2.6× | 1280 = 1.3× | 44% |
| 1920×1080 | 1.21 | 2.0× | 960 = 1.0× | 25% |

The project's own finding points the same way: at softness 80% a halved frame texture is practically identical in screenshots, so the top mip level of the 4K frame is not reaching the eye. What 4K still buys is chroma at twice the displayed resolution and insurance if the screen is enlarged. What it costs is 2.3 to 4 times the encoder and decoder work and correspondingly fewer bits per retained pixel. Two caveats keep this honest. Sunshine's host-side scaler is plain bilinear with no mipmaps ([Sunshine repository, display_vram.cpp](https://github.com/LizardByte/Sunshine)), so a 4K-to-1440p stream is filtered worse on the host than the phone filters it now; 4K-to-1080p is an exact 2:1 and clean. And the game must keep rendering at 4K, or the test measures lost supersampling rather than stream resolution. The user rejected a lower stream resolution for FC 27 before, and that judgement stands unless a like-for-like A/B (same softness in angular terms, same bitrate, game still at 4K) shows no loss; the list below sets one up.

## Conclusion

The research changes where effort should go. The downscale filter, which absorbed today's tuning, is finished: it exceeds the open-source Quest clients, and the user's soft, unsharpened preference is the same conclusion Virtual Desktop users and ALVR's developers reached about post-decode sharpening. The picture's remaining defects divide cleanly into three owners. The grid belongs to the panel and lens, and only film, focus or focal length touches it. Dimmed fine detail and colour fringes belong to two renderer steps that Cardboard-class software has never done. Banding and encode-time spikes belong to bit depth and GPU headroom on the host.

The uncomfortable implication is that the single change most likely to be seen is a piece of film or a lens swap costing a few dollars, and that the strongest software candidate, linear-light mips, is one texture-format change. Each of the three owners has at least one experiment that costs under an hour, so the ranking below orders by expected visible benefit per unit effort rather than by benefit alone.

## Ranked experiments

Conventions for the A/B column. "Split" means `adb shell setprop debug.vrdesktop.split 1`: real settings on the screen's left half, property overrides on the right. "Shots" means `scripts/vr-ab.sh` with a variants file (it sets `freeze_pose=1`, waits for the app's confirmation log line and saves a screenshot per variant, then runs `scripts/vr-ab-analyze.py`). Existing keys are `lod_bias`, `hq_lod`, `hq_spread`, `hq_sharp`, `sharp`, `aniso`, `frame_half`, `hq`, `asd`. Keys named "new" do not exist yet and are suggestions. **Screenshots capture the framebuffer before the lens and the PenTile panel**, so they prove what the shader produced but cannot judge grid, fringing or optical softness; items marked "eye only" must be decided in the headset. Stream-side changes need a reconnect and cannot be live-switched, so compare them on a paused replay or a still desktop with the pose frozen.

Not worth trying on this hardware, per the encoder research: lens-pre-warped stereo rendered on the host, foveated QP maps, a separate NVENC application, AV1 and 4:4:4. Each costs weeks or is blocked by the decoder, and each is expected to give no gain while bitrate is plentiful. A better downscale filter in Sunshine's conversion shader becomes worthwhile only if item 6 lands on 1440p.
